# Foundational documents for the `aboriginal_australian` anchor

Source texts the `aboriginal_australian` constitution is distilled from. The bundle combines First Nations accounts of Country, law, relationality, custodianship, and decoloniality with carefully bounded interpretive sources on Dreaming, landscape, and Aboriginal relationships with Country.

## Source coverage

### Bawaka Country et al. — *Caring as Country*

This source provides a Yolŋu account of **co-becoming**, in which humans are part of Country rather than separate managers of it. It develops *wetj* as relational sharing and emphasizes more-than-human agency, mutual care, attention, responsibility, obligation, and the idea that Country both cares and is cared for.

### Deborah Bird Rose — *Nourishing Terrains*

Rose provides a broad interpretive survey of Aboriginal relationships with Country through sacred geography, Dreaming ecology, knowledge, land management, caring for Country, and human and ecological rights. It is useful for coverage across themes, while remaining an interpretive synthesis rather than a single authoritative Aboriginal worldview.

### Irene Watson — *Re-Centring First Nations Knowledge and Places in a Terra Nullius Space*

Watson centers First Nations law, philosophy, knowledge, and obligations to care for Country while analyzing the continuing effects of terra nullius and colonial dispossession. She stresses that Indigenous knowledges are living, relational, locally grounded, and held by the First Nations peoples of particular countries.

### W. E. H. Stanner — *The Dreaming*

Stanner's essay is included as a historical interpretive account of The Dreaming as sacred ordering, law, continuity, and a framework connecting people, society, Country, totems, ancestors, and the present. Because Stanner was a non-Indigenous anthropologist writing in the mid-twentieth century, this source should supplement rather than override contemporary First Nations accounts.

### Mary Graham — *Some Thoughts about the Philosophical Underpinnings of Aboriginal Worldviews*

Graham gives the corpus its clearest general philosophical formulation through the precepts **“The Land is the Law”** and **“You are not alone in the world.”** She connects land, Dreaming, custodianship, kinship, sharing, collective reflection, long-term responsibility, and social relations, with relationships to land providing the foundation for relationships among people.

## Corpus perspective

Together, the sources emphasize **Country as foundational to law and meaning, relationality among human and more-than-human beings, custodianship and responsibility, kinship, reciprocity and sharing, Dreaming, sacred place, long-term care, living Indigenous knowledge, and resistance to colonial separation of people from land**.

This is intentionally a **plural corpus**, not a claim that Aboriginal Australian peoples share a single homogeneous philosophy. Aboriginal laws and knowledges are localized and held in relation to particular peoples and countries. The Bawaka Country, Watson, and Graham sources therefore carry particular importance as First Nations-centered accounts, while Rose and Stanner are best treated as interpretive and contextual sources.

## Licensing and redistribution

All five files are marked `redistributable: false`.

`Nourishing_Terrains(1).md` explicitly states © Commonwealth of Australia 1996 and “all rights reserved,” and the Graham source likewise states that all rights are reserved. Watson's published article carries a publisher copyright. *Caring as Country* is a copyrighted Wiley journal article for which no open redistribution license was identified.

Stanner's *White Man Got No Dreaming* is now available through the ANU Press open-access archive, but open access to read the work does not by itself establish permission to redistribute the extracted text. Because no explicit reuse license was identified for this edition, `The_Dreaming_pages_23-40(1).md` is conservatively kept internal.

The manifest and README may remain public so that the provenance and intellectual structure of the Aboriginal Australian anchor are documented even though the underlying source files are excluded from public redistribution.
