# Foundational documents for the `conservatism` anchor

Source texts the `conservatism` constitution is distilled from. The bundle combines Edmund Burke's defense of inherited political and social order, David Hume's skeptical and experience-based approach to political authority, and Alexis de Tocqueville's analysis of the institutions, associations, customs, and moral habits that sustain liberty in democratic societies.

The corpus does not define conservatism simply as resistance to change. Across the three authors, it instead emphasizes prudence, institutional continuity, accumulated social knowledge, skepticism toward abstract political reconstruction, local and intermediary institutions, civic obligation, social cohesion, ordered liberty, and attention to the unintended consequences of political action.

## Selection decisions

The three authors provide complementary dimensions of the `conservatism` anchor.

- **Edmund Burke, *Reflections on the Revolution in France*** — supplies the corpus's strongest account of inherited order, political prudence, historical continuity, and skepticism toward revolutionary reconstruction. Burke argues that political principles cannot be evaluated adequately in abstraction from circumstance and that liberty must coexist with government, order, morality, property, and durable social institutions. He treats political society as an inheritance developed across generations rather than as an arrangement that each generation should reconstruct anew from first principles. His work also emphasizes precedent, prescription, gradual reform, institutional continuity, and the dangers of destroying accumulated social knowledge in pursuit of theoretical purity.

- **David Hume, selected political essays** — provides a more skeptical and empirically oriented account of political authority. Hume questions highly abstract accounts of legitimacy, particularly attempts to explain existing political obligation through speculative theories disconnected from historical practice. His essays examine consent, obedience, political parties, succession, constitutional arrangements, and the practical foundations of government. This contributes an emphasis on convention, experience, moderation, institutional stability, and close attention to how political societies actually arise and function rather than how an ideal theory says they ought to have arisen.

- **Alexis de Tocqueville, *Democracy in America*** — broadens the corpus from political authority and institutional inheritance to the social conditions required for a free society to remain viable. Tocqueville examines local government, decentralization, voluntary associations, religion, civic participation, social habits, individualism, materialism, majority power, and administrative centralization. His work is particularly important for the idea that liberty depends upon institutions and practices between the isolated individual and the centralized state.

## How the sources complement one another

The sources approach conservative political thought from three different directions.

**Burke** supplies prudence, inheritance, historical continuity, and skepticism toward revolutionary political reconstruction.

**Hume** supplies epistemic skepticism, convention, political moderation, and an insistence that institutions be evaluated in light of experience and actual human behavior.

**Tocqueville** supplies localism, civil society, voluntary association, moral and religious habits, and an analysis of how democratic individualism and centralization can weaken the social foundations of liberty.

Together, they produce a form of conservatism that is not reducible to preserving every existing institution. Existing arrangements can be defective and reform can be necessary. The common constraint is that political reform should take seriously accumulated experience, institutional interdependence, social context, human limitations, and the possibility that destroying imperfect institutions may also destroy functions that are difficult to recreate deliberately.

## Major dimensions represented in the corpus

### Prudence and circumstance

Burke places circumstance at the center of political judgment. Political principles do not operate in isolation, and the consequences of a reform depend upon the institutions, practices, incentives, and social conditions surrounding it.

This makes prudence more than simple caution. Political action should consider likely consequences, institutional dependencies, historical experience, and the limits of prediction before disrupting arrangements that coordinate large numbers of people.

### Inheritance and continuity

Political and social institutions embody knowledge accumulated across generations. Burke's language of inheritance expresses the idea that people receive institutions, rights, obligations, customs, and practices that they did not individually create.

This does not make inherited institutions immune from criticism. It does create a presumption in favor of understanding their purposes and dependencies before replacing them.

### Skepticism toward abstract political construction

Both Burke and Hume resist the idea that a political society can be adequately reconstructed from a small set of abstract principles.

Hume's critique is especially empirical: theories of political authority must be compared with the historical origins and actual functioning of governments. Burke emphasizes that even valuable ideals such as liberty acquire their practical meaning through particular circumstances and institutions.

The corpus therefore favors political reasoning that moves between principles, history, experience, and consequences rather than deriving institutional arrangements mechanically from theory.

### Political authority, order, and liberty

The corpus rejects both unrestricted political power and the idea that freedom consists simply in the absence of authority.

Burke treats durable liberty as dependent upon functioning institutions and social order. Hume examines why political authority and obedience arise and persist. Tocqueville shows how authority can be distributed through local institutions so that government remains effective without becoming excessively centralized.

The resulting emphasis is on **ordered liberty**: preserving meaningful freedom while maintaining the institutional capacity required for law, cooperation, stability, and public life.

### Local government and decentralization

Tocqueville's treatment of American townships gives local government a particularly important role in the corpus. Local political institutions allow citizens to participate directly in common affairs, learn the practice of self-government, develop attachments to their communities, and experience the relationship between private and public interests.

Decentralization also distributes power rather than merely weakening it. Authority can remain effective while its exercise is divided among institutions closer to the people and circumstances affected by it.

### Civil society and associations

Tocqueville emphasizes voluntary associations and shared civic activity as responses to democratic individualism.

A society composed only of isolated individuals and a centralized state is vulnerable to political passivity and administrative domination. Associations, communities, local institutions, religious organizations, and other intermediary bodies create relationships through which people cooperate without relying exclusively on centralized government.

### Social cohesion and individualism

Tocqueville distinguishes democratic individualism from simple selfishness. Democratic societies can encourage people to withdraw into private life, weaken intergenerational and communal ties, and become less conscious of their dependence upon others.

Free institutions and associations counteract this tendency by repeatedly drawing citizens into common projects and making mutual dependence visible.

### Religion, morality, and non-material goods

Tocqueville also examines the importance of religious and moral beliefs in limiting an exclusive focus on material well-being.

Within the corpus, this contributes a broader conservative concern that social orders depend upon motivations, loyalties, obligations, and conceptions of human flourishing that cannot always be generated through law, markets, or administrative institutions alone.

### Centralization and administrative dependence

Tocqueville distinguishes political authority from administrative centralization. A society may require strong laws and effective government without placing the administration of every local matter in a single centralized bureaucracy.

His criticism of excessive centralization is partly epistemic: central authorities cannot possess detailed knowledge of every local circumstance. It is also civic: replacing citizens' own activity with centralized administration can diminish their ability and willingness to govern themselves.

## Interpretive works

No separate modern interpretive work is included.

The corpus instead combines three historically distinct but complementary thinkers. Burke provides a foundational conservative response to revolutionary rationalism; Hume provides an earlier philosophical basis for political skepticism and institutional conventionalism; and Tocqueville analyzes how conservative concerns about social cohesion, intermediary institutions, civic habits, and centralized power operate within a democratic society.

Because none of the three is treated as an exhaustive definition of conservatism, the constitution should be distilled from areas of convergence while preserving genuine tensions among their views.

Project Gutenberg identifies the underlying work as **public domain in the United States**. The corpus file contains material selected for relevance to the conservatism anchor while preserving the substantive wording and source order of the retained passages.

Project Gutenberg headers, footers, navigation material, and distribution boilerplate may be removed from the research corpus.

### David Hume

`10 Essay.md` is derived from **Project Gutenberg eBook #59792, *Hume's Political Discourses***, edited by William Bell Robertson.

The source contains political essays addressing the original contract, political obedience, party conflict, succession, constitutional arrangements, the possibility of a science of politics, the foundations of government, and political society. The Project Gutenberg edition explicitly permits copying and reuse under its terms and identifies the underlying text as public domain in the United States.

The file is used as the Hume component of the conservatism corpus rather than as a claim that every political or economic essay written by Hume belongs to a distinctly conservative doctrine.

### Alexis de Tocqueville

The Tocqueville material is divided between two files:

- `Conservatism_Critique_25k_relevant_to_DIA.md` draws from **Volume I** of *Democracy in America* and concentrates on political and institutional themes such as townships, local government, decentralization, authority, law, majority power, and civic participation. The retained material explicitly describes the township as a setting in which citizens learn government, order, rights, duties, and the balance of powers.
- `Conservatism_Critique_15k_relevant_to_DIA.md` draws from **Volume II** and concentrates more heavily on democratic social life, including individualism, associations, religion, materialism, civic participation, and the dangers created when citizens become isolated from one another.

The underlying English translation by Henry Reeve was obtained from **Project Gutenberg eBooks #815 and #816**, both public domain in the United States.
