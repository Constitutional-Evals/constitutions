# Foundational documents for the `universal_kindness` anchor

Source texts the `universal_kindness` constitution is distilled from. The bundle is intentionally cross-traditional rather than representing a single philosophical or religious school. It combines Mohist impartial care, early Buddhist universal goodwill and compassion, Jane Addams's social ethics, Adam Smith's moral psychology of sympathy and impartial judgment, and the universal dignity and rights articulated in the *Universal Declaration of Human Rights*.

Together, these sources treat kindness not simply as personal niceness or emotional sympathy, but as a broader orientation of goodwill, practical concern, respectful treatment, social responsibility, non-harm, perspective-taking, and recognition of the dignity and legitimate interests of others.

## Selection decisions

The five sources play complementary roles in defining the `universal_kindness` anchor.

- **Mozi, *Universal Love I–III*** — provides the strongest explicit argument for extending concern beyond narrow circles of self, family, and political group. Mohist universal love is practical rather than merely emotional: it concerns how people treat, assist, protect, and refrain from harming one another. The chapters connect partiality with conflict and disorder while presenting more impartial concern as a basis for mutual benefit, peace, and responsible social organization.

- **Early Buddhist sources** — provide the corpus's strongest account of kindness as a cultivated disposition toward all beings. The selected texts develop universal goodwill, compassion toward suffering, sympathetic joy in others' flourishing, and equanimity. They also address kindness under difficult conditions: provocation, criticism, hostility, harmful speech, resentment, and conflict. This material makes non-hatred, harmlessness, truthful and beneficial speech, and disciplined concern for oneself and others central elements of the anchor.

- **Jane Addams, *Democracy and Social Ethics*** — extends kindness from interpersonal conduct into social relationships and institutions. Addams emphasizes learning to understand people from within circumstances different from one's own, treating others as participants rather than passive recipients of benevolence, and recognizing how labor, poverty, family arrangements, politics, and social structures shape ethical responsibility. Her work is especially important for distinguishing kindness from paternalistic charity and for connecting goodwill with democratic fellowship, reciprocity, and structural concern.

- **Adam Smith, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*** — supplies a detailed moral psychology of sympathy and perspective-taking. Smith examines how people imaginatively enter into the situations of others while also correcting partial or impulsive reactions through a more impartial standpoint. His treatment of sympathy, beneficence, justice, propriety, resentment, self-command, duty, and the impartial spectator helps connect kindness with accurate understanding, appropriate emotional response, fairness, and moral restraint.

- **The *Universal Declaration of Human Rights*** — provides a universal framework of dignity, equality, freedom, and basic rights. It establishes important constraints on benevolent action: concern for someone's welfare does not license coercion, discrimination, degradation, suppression of conscience, or disregard for autonomy. It also broadens the corpus beyond interpersonal kindness to include political participation, social and economic security, education, cultural participation, and the conditions necessary for people to live with dignity.

## How the sources complement one another

The corpus deliberately combines several different conceptions of concern for others.

**Mozi** supplies impartiality and practical concern.  
**Early Buddhism** supplies universal goodwill, compassion, and non-hatred.  
**Addams** supplies relational humility and social responsibility.  
**Smith** supplies sympathy, perspective-taking, and impartial moral judgment.  
**The UDHR** supplies dignity, equality, autonomy, and rights-based limits.

No single source is therefore treated as a complete definition of universal kindness. The anchor is intended to emerge from their overlap and productive tensions. In particular, the corpus can distinguish universal moral concern from identical treatment: all persons or beings may deserve goodwill and consideration even when relationships, responsibilities, needs, circumstances, or competing rights justify different practical responses.

## Interpretive works

No separate modern interpretive work is included as an independent source.

The corpus instead draws its conceptual breadth from the interaction of multiple traditions and genres: classical Chinese moral and political philosophy, early Buddhist ethical and contemplative teachings, social ethics, eighteenth-century moral philosophy, and a modern international declaration of human rights.

This reduces dependence on any single modern commentator while still supplying multiple perspectives from which concepts such as benevolence, compassion, sympathy, dignity, impartiality, social responsibility, and respect for autonomy can be interpreted.
