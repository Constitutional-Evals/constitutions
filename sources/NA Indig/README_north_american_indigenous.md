# Foundational documents for the `north_american_indigenous` anchor

Source texts the `north_american_indigenous` constitution is distilled from. The bundle brings together Potawatomi, Anishinaabek/Nishnaabeg, and multi-nation perspectives on relationships with land and other beings, Indigenous knowledge and law, reciprocity, self-determination, decolonization, climate justice, and the protection of sacred places.

## Source coverage

### Kyle Whyte — *Indigenous Climate Change Studies*

Whyte connects contemporary climate change to longer histories of colonial environmental disruption. He emphasizes Indigenous knowledge renewal, self-determined adaptation, relationships with human and nonhuman relatives, collective memory, reciprocity, and responsibilities toward future generations.

### Deborah McGregor — *Indigenous Environmental Justice, Knowledge, and Law*

McGregor grounds environmental justice in Indigenous knowledge systems, legal orders, governance, and sovereignty rather than merely adding Indigenous perspectives to existing Western frameworks. Her Anishinaabek-centered account emphasizes responsibilities among people, land, waters, ancestors, future generations, the spirit world, and other beings in Creation.

### Robin Wall Kimmerer — *Returning the Gift*

Kimmerer provides the corpus's clearest account of gratitude and reciprocity with the living Earth. Drawing on Potawatomi teachings, she links gifts to responsibilities, challenges human exceptionalism, calls for restraint and ecological restoration, and treats humans as participants in reciprocal relationships with other living beings.

### Leanne Betasamosake Simpson — *Land as Pedagogy*

Simpson develops land as both the setting and process through which Nishnaabeg knowledge, values, agency, and political life are generated. The article emphasizes embodied and relational learning, family and community, consent, animal and plant teachers, self-determination, and resurgence beyond assimilative settler-colonial institutions.

### *The Sacred Land Reader*

The Reader broadens the corpus through multiple Native American cases involving sacred geography, religious freedom, law, land management, and resistance to extraction or development. Its essays emphasize that relationships to sacred places are cultural, spiritual, historical, and political obligations that cannot be reduced to ordinary property interests.

## Corpus perspective

Together, the sources emphasize **reciprocity and responsibility, relationality with land and other beings, gratitude, Indigenous knowledge and legal orders, self-determination, community and intergenerational obligations, protection of sacred places, decolonization, environmental and climate justice, and the renewal of relationships damaged by settler colonialism**.

This is intentionally a **plural corpus**, not a claim that North American Indigenous peoples share a single homogeneous philosophy. The sources come from distinct nations, authors, and contexts, and the anchor should preserve that diversity rather than treating any one formulation as universally representative.
